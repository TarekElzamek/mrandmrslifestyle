Why You're Not Building Muscle (The Real Reason)

By Tarek Elzamek  ·  5 min read  ·  Updated October 2026
← Back to Blog

You're in the gym consistently. You're eating enough. You're taking your protein. But the muscle isn't coming — or it's coming so slowly it's invisible. Sound familiar?

After coaching hundreds of men at Mr & Mrs Lifestyle, Tarek has identified the real reasons most men plateau — and they're almost never what people expect.

Mistake 1: Training Without Progressive Overload

This is the single biggest reason men stop building muscle. Progressive overload means consistently increasing the demand on your muscles over time — more weight, more reps, better technique, shorter rest periods.

Most men go to the gym, lift the same weights in the same rep ranges, week after week. Their body adapts within a few weeks and stops responding. If the workout isn't getting harder, your muscles aren't getting bigger.

Tarek's Rule

Every session, aim to beat your previous performance in at least one exercise. One extra rep, 2.5kg more weight, or a cleaner range of motion. Consistent small improvements compound into major results.

Mistake 2: Not Eating Enough Protein

The research on this is clear: you need 1.8–2.2g of protein per kg of bodyweight to maximise muscle protein synthesis. For an 80kg man, that's 144–176g of protein per day. Most men eating "a lot of protein" are actually hitting 80–100g. That's not enough.

Protein isn't just a supplement — it's the literal building material of muscle. Without enough of it, you simply cannot build at the rate your training warrants.

Mistake 3: Insufficient Sleep and Recovery

Muscle is not built in the gym. It's built while you sleep. Growth hormone is released primarily during deep sleep — which is when your muscle fibres actually repair and grow larger. Consistently sleeping less than 7 hours significantly reduces your anabolic hormone profile and increases cortisol, both of which directly limit muscle growth.

The Fix

Prioritise 7-9 hours of sleep. Take at least one full rest day between training sessions for the same muscle group. Eat in a slight calorie surplus if muscle gain is your primary goal. And above all — measure your progress so you can see whether what you're doing is working.

Mistake 4: Program-Hopping

A new routine every two weeks feels exciting, but your body never gets the chance to adapt and improve at anything. Muscle grows when you get stronger at the same movements over time, and you can only measure that if you stick with them.

Pick a well-structured programme and run it for at least 8–12 weeks. Keep your main exercises the same and make them progressively heavier. Change accessory exercises if you get bored, not your entire plan.

Mistake 5: Not Enough Training Volume (or Far Too Much)

Volume means the number of hard sets you do per muscle each week. Too little and there isn't enough stimulus to grow. Too much and you can't recover between sessions. A practical starting point for most men is around 10–20 hard sets per muscle group per week, spread over two or more sessions.

"Hard" is the important word. A set only counts if it ends close to failure, roughly one to three reps short of not being able to do another. Ten easy sets do less than five genuinely challenging ones.

Tarek's Rule

Count your hard sets per muscle for one week. Most men who've stopped growing are either doing 6 sets for their back or 30 for their chest. Both need fixing.

Mistake 6: Eating Too Little (or Dirty Bulking)

You can't build much muscle in a big calorie deficit. But eating everything in sight won't help either: most of that extra weight ends up as fat. The best approach for most men is a small surplus of around 200–300 calories a day, aiming to gain roughly 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per month (a little faster if you're a beginner).

If you're carrying more body fat than you'd like, a body recomposition approach can work: eat around maintenance, hit your protein every day and train hard. You'll lose fat and build muscle at the same time, especially if you're newer to proper training.

Mistake 7: Poor Exercise Selection and Technique

Exercises that let you train a muscle through a long range of motion, under control, tend to build the most muscle. Ego lifting with half reps and swinging does not.

  • Chest: pressing variations with a full, controlled range of motion.
  • Back: rows and pulldowns where you pause and squeeze, instead of yanking the weight.
  • Legs: squats, leg presses, lunges and Romanian deadlifts taken through a deep range you can control.
  • Arms and shoulders: controlled curls, extensions and lateral raises, rather than heavy cheating.

A Simple Weekly Plan to Start Building Muscle

If you've been stuck, an upper/lower split four days a week is a reliable way to restart progress:

  • Day 1, Upper: bench press, row, overhead press, pulldown, arms.
  • Day 2, Lower: squat, Romanian deadlift, leg press or lunge, calves.
  • Day 3: rest or walk.
  • Day 4, Upper: incline press, chest-supported row, lateral raise, pull-ups, arms.
  • Day 5, Lower: deadlift or hip thrust, front squat or hack squat, leg curl, calves.

Log every session. When you hit the top of your rep range on all sets, add weight next time.

The Bottom Line

Building muscle isn't mysterious. It's a predictable biological response to consistent stimulus, adequate nutrition, and sufficient recovery. If any one of those three pillars is missing, your results will stall — regardless of how hard you're trying. Get all three right, and progress becomes almost inevitable.

Frequently Asked Questions

How fast can a man build muscle naturally?

Beginners can gain muscle quickly in their first year of proper training. After that progress slows, which is why progressive overload, protein and recovery become even more important.

How much protein do I need to build muscle?

Around 1.8 to 2.2g per kg of bodyweight per day. For an 80kg man that's roughly 144 to 176g of protein.

Do I need supplements to build muscle?

No. Food, training and sleep do the heavy lifting. Protein powder and creatine can be convenient and useful, but they're not essential.

How many days a week should I train to build muscle?

Three to five days works for most people. Four days, using an upper/lower split, is a good balance of training and recovery.
